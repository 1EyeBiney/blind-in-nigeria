# Cataract Surgery in Ibadan: Who Does It, What It Costs, Whether There Is Room for More, and Who Else Is Working on It

Prepared for Brian, September 22, 2026. Two research passes (files NG8 and NG9 in the research folder), sources checked that day. This note says plainly what is confirmed, what is inferred, and what only a direct inquiry can settle.

## First, a correction to memory

The provider doing about 3,300 cataract surgeries a year is not the university hospital. It is Eleta Eye Institute, a private not-for-profit eye hospital network based in Ibadan with five sites, which reported 16,590 cataract surgeries over five years. University College Hospital Ibadan, the teaching hospital, has an active ophthalmology department and does cataract surgery, but no volume figure for it was found. Both matter; they are different institutions.

## Eleta Eye Institute: what is known

Eleta is led by Dr. B. G. K. Ajayi, who is also chairman of the Eleta Eye Foundation, its charitable arm. It is accredited by Nigeria's postgraduate medical colleges to train ophthalmology residents, which means it is a teaching institution, not only a clinic. In 2025 it opened, with Christian Blind Mission and HelpMeSee, Nigeria's first virtual-reality simulation centre for training cataract surgeons in manual small-incision cataract surgery, the low-cost technique that needs no expensive machines, reliable electricity, or disposables, and the technique behind every "hundred-dollar surgery" figure in the world. The announcement framed the centre as a response to a public-health crisis of untreated cataract.

What is not known, because Eleta's foundation website was offline for maintenance both times it was checked: whether the foundation publishes accounts or a board, whether it has a sponsorship price per surgery, how a foreign donor sponsors surgeries, and what share of its surgeries go to patients who cannot pay. The surgical volume is Eleta's own figure, not an audited one. Its only working public channels found were its Facebook and Instagram pages. All of this is one email away, and that email is the single most useful next step in this whole area.

## What a cataract surgery costs, and where the $100 came from

The honest answer is that nobody has published a per-surgery price for Ibadan, and the peer-reviewed Nigerian cost studies that would settle it were blocked behind bot checks in both passes, so they were not read. What was found, in rough order from the cheapest claim to the dearest:

Orbis, an international eye charity, tells donors an adult cataract operation costs "as little as £24" (about $32), country unspecified. Sightsavers raised £32 million for "a million cataract operations," a campaign ratio of about £32 ($43) each, not an itemized unit cost. Cure Blindness Project prices a surgery at $70, but does not work in Nigeria. Unite For Sight, the source of the $100 figure carried through our earlier documents, prices a sponsored surgery at $100 and works in "Africa and Asia" without naming Nigeria. Tulsi Chanrai Foundation, an Indian-Nigerian family foundation, gives cataract surgery entirely free at its own hospitals and camps in Nigeria; its cost per surgery is not published. At the other end, Nigerian private-sector price lists put cataract surgery at 400,000 to 1,000,000 naira, $260 to $650, and a patient at a subsidized teaching-hospital outreach in Benin City paid 10,000 naira, about $7, with the sponsor covering the rest.

So the $100 is a reasonable planning figure with a real basis, the manual small-incision technique that Eleta itself teaches, but it is an international charity benchmark, not an Ibadan price. A fair range to carry until Eleta or another Nigerian provider quotes is $70 to $130 per surgery for a sponsored indigent patient, and the church should not put a number in front of donors without a written quote.

## Is there capacity to do more?

The claim in our earlier report rests on inference, not on a measured waiting list. Here is the inference, in the open. Nigeria's cataract surgical rate is about 317 surgeries per million people per year, far below what is needed to keep up with new cases, let alone clear the backlog; the national survey found 84 percent of blindness avoidable and the 2022 Saki East survey in rural Oyo found 94.6 percent avoidable, which means the untreated people exist. Eleta and its partners chose to build a surgeon-training centre, which is what an institution does when it believes demand exceeds surgeons. The state's SightQuest program was funded to expand cataract surgery. And across Nigeria, subsidized outreach camps routinely draw hundreds of patients in a few days, which is what unmet demand looks like on the ground.

What that does not prove is that Eleta, this year, has operating-room time and surgeons sitting idle for want of paying patients. It may; hospitals running at 3,300 a year often can add cases if someone covers the indigent patient's costs, because the marginal surgery is cheap once the theatre and staff exist. But the only way to know is to ask: how many patients who cannot pay do you see, turn away, or wait-list; what does it cost you to operate on one more of them; and would a sponsor covering that cost let you do more. Those are the questions for Eleta, and the answers are the numbers the church needs.

One barrier is on the patient's side, not the hospital's. Studies across Nigeria find that fear, cost, distance, and the belief that nothing can be done keep people from surgery even when it is free. Money alone does not fill a theatre; someone has to find the people and bring them, which is exactly the role a local helper in Ibadan, the one we have discussed for the screen-reader work, could also play.

## Nigerian organizations a small church could contact

Eleta Eye Institute and Foundation, Ibadan: the natural first call, for the reasons above; reachable through its Facebook and Instagram pages until its website returns.

Tulsi Chanrai Foundation: the strongest confirmed model of free cataract surgery at scale in Nigeria, donor-funded, with a published phone number and email (info@tcfnigeria.org); it operates its own eye hospitals and camps. Worth asking whether it works in Oyo State and whether it accepts designated gifts for camps there.

Christian Blind Mission (CBM) Nigeria: Christian-heritage, active in Nigeria, and a partner in the Ibadan training centre. One due-diligence note in its favor: CBM was independently reported to have suspended a partner hospital elsewhere in Nigeria over fraud, which is what a funder with real controls does.

Sightsavers Nigeria: large, secular, active, with a donor contact address; more likely to take a general gift than a designated one.

ECWA Eye Hospital, Kano: a Christian mission eye hospital founded in 1943 with a stated free-surgery program, far from Ibadan but a model and a possible partner for a gospel-explicit church.

The Ophthalmological Society of Nigeria: the professional body, useful for finding an Ibadan surgeon who runs outreach.

## American organizations already working on this

The most useful finding: CharityVision, also known as Vision Outreach International, a US organization, runs a live, named Nigeria program with a "Donate to Nigeria" option, reported 1,019 surgeries and 2,076 pairs of glasses across 32 outreaches in 2024, and set a 2026 goal of 3,000 surgeries. It does not publish a per-surgery price, but it is the one American organization found through which a church can give money designated to cataract surgery in Nigeria today with a receipt from a US charity. Whether its Nigerian partners reach Oyo State is a question to ask it.

HelpMeSee, in New York, co-founded the Ibadan training centre, so it has a direct relationship with Eleta; its donation page does not name Nigeria or a price, which makes it worth a direct inquiry rather than a website donation. CBM USA and Sightsavers USA both fund Nigeria but offer general giving rather than a sponsor-a-surgery product. Samaritan's Purse, through World Medical Mission, supports ECWA Hospital Egbe in Kogi State, which has an eye department but no confirmed cataract program; a gift there would be to the hospital generally. SEE International has a Nigeria page but reports no current clinics or surgeries there. Cure Blindness Project and Mercy Ships do not currently work in Nigeria. Two searches with different wording found no Indiana church or organization with an eye-care mission in Nigeria; if the church does this, it will be the first in the state that we could find.

## What this means for the church

The first gift of sponsored surgeries remains a sound idea, but it needs one of two paths to become real. The direct path: write to Eleta with the three capacity questions and a request for a per-surgery sponsorship price, and, if the answers are good, fund a small number of surgeries under the church's overseas-gift agreement with a count and a photo back. The indirect path: give through CharityVision designated to Nigeria, which is simpler, receipted in the United States, and immediate, but which may not reach Oyo State and puts an organization between the church and the people. Doing the direct inquiry first costs nothing and tells the church which path it is on within a few weeks. Either way, the number that goes in front of the congregation should be a quoted one.

## Two short inquiries, ready to send

To Eleta Eye Institute: Our church in Indianapolis, which has several blind members and a family connection to Ibadan, would like to sponsor cataract surgeries for patients who cannot pay. Could you tell us what one sponsored surgery costs at Eleta, how many patients who cannot pay you see or wait-list in a typical month, whether a sponsor's gift would let you operate on more of them, and how you would confirm to us the surgeries that a gift paid for?

To CharityVision: We are a small church in Indiana considering a gift designated to your Nigeria program. Could you tell us which Nigerian partners and states your outreaches reach, whether any are in Oyo State or Ibadan, roughly what one cataract surgery costs through your program, and what a donor receives afterward?
