# How Blind Nigerians Earn a Living: What the Evidence Actually Says

Prepared October 28, 2026. The question: what does the evidence say about how blind adults in Nigeria earn a living, and in particular whether any defensible figure exists for the share who beg. The sentence "70 percent of blind people in Nigeria have to beg to survive" had been used in conversation; it is withdrawn, and this note records what can and cannot be said instead. Research detail, with every URL and access date, is in research/NG11-livelihoods-data.md. This note is written to stand on its own for a reader outside the project and names no private individuals.

## Bottom line

No study, survey, or government source supports any national percentage of blind Nigerians who beg; the often-repeated numbers come from one small city study and one untraceable 1972 conference paper. The best primary datum is a 2010 study in Ilorin in which 47 of 61 totally blind adults (77 percent) lived by street begging and 89 percent wanted other work, but the sample was recruited partly at begging sites in one city and cannot speak for Nigeria. The famous "70 percent of Lagos's 4,000 beggars were blind" is a claim about beggars, not about blind people, and traces to a 1972 conference proceedings paper whose original cannot be found or checked. The one clinically measured modern value points the other way: among 168 destitute people examined in Onitsha in 2011, 12.5 percent were blind. What national data does show is a steep, well-documented disadvantage: Nigerian adults with a severe functional difficulty are employed at 38 percent against 60 percent for everyone else, blindness is more than five times as common in the poorest households as in the most affluent, and blind street beggars who have been studied are overwhelmingly without formal education and without access to rehabilitation. The honest picture is not "most blind Nigerians beg," which nobody has measured, but that blindness and deep poverty travel together, begging is a documented survival strategy where rehabilitation is absent, and nearly everyone studied who begs wants other work.

## Three claims that get blended, and what supports each

The share of blind people who beg. Measured once, in Ilorin in 2010: 77 percent of a 61-person convenience sample. No population-based estimate exists for any city or for Nigeria.

The share of beggars who are blind. Three data points, and they disagree: Lagos 1972, about 70 percent of roughly 4,000 beggars (untraceable conference paper, unknown method); Abakaliki 2019, 15 of 50 beggars (30 percent) self-reported sight impairment in a tiny purposive sample; Onitsha 2011, 12.5 percent of 168 clinically examined destitute individuals were blind, with another 10.2 percent visually impaired (the only clinically measured value, though "destitute" is broader than "beggar"). The spread from 12.5 to 70 percent shows there is no stable figure; the share plainly varies by city, era, and definition.

The share of blind adults without paid work. Never measured directly for blind Nigerians. The nearest proxies: employment-to-population of 38 percent for adults with at least "a lot of difficulty" in any functional domain versus 60 percent without (2018 General Household Survey Panel, not seeing-specific); unemployment at ages 25 to 64 of 61 percent for people with disabilities versus 21 percent without (Leonard Cheshire analysis of GHS Panel data, all disabilities).

## What a defensible anchor sentence could be

Candidate one, the honest local figure: "In the one published study of blind adults in a Nigerian city, 77 percent of the 61 people interviewed survived by street begging, and 89 percent of them wanted other work." Source: Ademola-Popoola, Tunde-Ayinmode and Akande, Middle East African Journal of Ophthalmology 2010, PMC2991452. Use when talking about begging; the second half is the part that matters for our message.

Candidate one-b, the stronger version of the same point: "When 202 blind street beggars in Sokoto were asked, 201 of them, 99.5 percent, said they wanted another way to earn a living, half of them in farming or trade." Source: Balarabe, Mahmoud, Omolase and Nasiru, Nigerian Journal of Clinical Practice 2014;17(5):637-640, PMID 25244277. This is the largest sample ever asked, and it says the same thing as Ilorin with near-unanimity: begging is a last resort, not a choice.

Candidate two, the national economic gap: "Nigerian adults with a severe disability are employed at 38 percent, against 60 percent for everyone else, and are far more likely to live in deep poverty." Source: Disability Data Initiative Nigeria brief, 2018 General Household Survey Panel. Use when a national, current, defensible number is needed; note it covers all severe functional difficulty, not blindness alone.

Candidate three, the blindness-poverty link: "Blindness in Nigeria is more than five times as common in the poorest households as in the most affluent ones." Source: Tafida et al., poverty paper of the Nigeria National Blindness and Visual Impairment Survey 2005-2007 (prevalence 8.5 percent versus 1.5 percent). This is the strongest blindness-specific national statistic that exists.

Not defensible, retired: "70 percent of blind people in Nigeria have to beg to survive" (never stated by any source); "70 percent of Lagos beggars are blind" (1972, untraceable); "90 percent of blind Nigerians are not working" (unsourced fundraising copy).

## The American contrast, and what the rising US rate means

The right comparison with the United States is not the employment rate but what unemployment means. Working-age blind and visually impaired Americans were employed at 52.3 percent in 2023 (American Community Survey, via the National Research and Training Center on Blindness and Low Vision at Mississippi State), against 76.3 percent for the general population. Nigerians with a severe functional difficulty of any kind are employed at 38 percent, and no blind-specific Nigerian rate exists. Those headline numbers are not different in kind; both describe steep odds. What differs is the floor under the person who is not working. An unemployed blind American typically has SSI or SSDI, Medicaid, a state vocational rehabilitation agency, subsidized cane and technology training, the ADA, and a free screen reader. An unemployed blind Nigerian has a 1.4 percent chance of any social protection coverage (Leonard Cheshire analysis cited by the World Bank's 2020 rapid assessment), rehabilitation that costs about N750,000 at the vocational center in Oshodi (ICIR, 2019), and begging as the documented fallback, which 99.5 percent of the 202 blind beggars asked in Sokoto wanted to leave. The same cliff, but one country has a net and the other has pavement.

The US rate has also risen sharply, and the shape of the rise matters. The ACS series ran 43.1 percent in 2008, fell to 36.3 percent in 2011 after the recession, recovered to 44.2 percent by 2017, and reached 52.3 percent in 2023, an eight-point climb in six years. The reasons given in the research and reporting: remote work removed commuting, the single hardest practical barrier for blind workers; a tight post-pandemic labor market pulled in workers employers had previously passed over; and 2023 set an all-time record for US disability employment generally (Bureau of Labor Statistics, reported by Axios, February 2024). Two honest caveats travel with it: the ACS identifies visual impairment by self-report, so the series includes people with milder vision loss, and the pandemic changed who counts as disabled, so part of the rise may be composition rather than opportunity. But the direction is real, and its lesson is the one this project is built on: when the environment changes, blind employment moves, which means the low numbers were never about capacity. Expectations and infrastructure are half the battle. That is the argument for showing what blind Nigerians already do and equipping them, rather than pitying them.

## What remains unknown

No one has measured what share of blind Nigerians beg, nationally or in any city, with a representative sample. No employment rate specific to people with seeing difficulty has been published from any Nigerian survey, though the microdata to compute one exists (2018 GHS Panel, DHS 2018, MICS 2021). The 2006 census count of visually impaired persons by type is not online. The Onitsha 2022 paper has now been read and its 12.5 percent figure included. The Sokoto alternative-livelihoods abstract and the Ibadan female-migrant-beggars full text were both recovered later the same day; the Sokoto figures are included above, and the Ibadan paper turned out to contain no blindness or disability data at all, so it adds context on Ibadan's begging economy (most of the 381 women surveyed earn N400-1,000 a day, against an area average near N2,500) but nothing to the blindness question. No official total or blind share exists for the 2021 Oyo Sabo evacuation, and our earlier "more than 3,000" should not be repeated without a source.
