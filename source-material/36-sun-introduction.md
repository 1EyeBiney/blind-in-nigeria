# Symbolic Universal Notation: An Introduction for People Who Know Braille

Prepared for Brian, October 16, 2026, to educate himself and to explain SUN to braille readers and teachers. Sources are in research/W2-sun-notation.md and W3-accessing-sun.md, with direct checks of sun.bible and the published SUN New Testament the same day. Everything about results is Wycliffe Associates' own account or press repeating it; no independent evaluation exists in public.

## The one thing to understand first

SUN was not built for blind people. It was built for Deaf people, and especially for Deaf people who have no language at all: no national sign language, no literacy, no schooling. Wycliffe Associates cites about 70 million Deaf people in the world, roughly 80 percent of whom know no sign language, and about 600,000 people who are both deaf and blind. SUN is a way to give those people Scripture in a form that bypasses spoken language entirely. The tactile version for Deaf-Blind readers, raised symbols produced on 3D printers, was described as "in progress" in 2020 and still in 2024, and nothing published describes SUN being used by blind people who can hear. For a hearing blind person, audio Scripture and screen readers already do what SUN does for the Deaf. This does not make SUN irrelevant to Brian; it means the connection runs through the Deaf-Blind, through the technology, and through what an accessibility specialist can contribute, not through blind Nigerians needing SUN instead of audio or braille.

## Where it came from

SUN was developed inside Wycliffe Associates in the late 2010s. Press accounts credit a volunteer (the organization's own pages name no designer) who worked for about four years on a symbol system drawing on the idea of Mandarin characters, prompted by the question of how a deaf-blind person with no language could ever receive Scripture; Bruce Smith described the result as the work of a team of Deaf and hearing people. About 400 online volunteers in the United States rendered the New Testament into SUN over roughly a year and a half, working from the Unlocked Literal Bible, an open-licensed English text. The SUN New Testament was dedicated in Orlando on February 20, 2020. The Old Testament was in final accuracy checks in January 2024. Fifty Bible stories, a catechism, and Our Daily Bread devotions also exist in SUN. The texts are published under a Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike license, which matters later.

## How it works, for a braille reader

Braille encodes a spoken language: each cell stands for a letter, a contraction, or a punctuation mark of English or Yoruba or Hausa, and a braille reader must already know the language. SUN encodes concepts, not words of any language. Each symbol stands for an idea: person, write, God, water, go. Symbols are built from a small set of geometric base characters, and base characters combine or are modified to form "extensions" for more specific meanings; the organization's own example is that the symbol for Person combined with the symbol for Write yields author, writer, or scribe. The published core is one hundred symbols (one source says eighty for the 3D version), and the organization states that core symbols plus their extensions can represent all of Scripture. Because SUN encodes meaning rather than sound, the same SUN page is read by a Deaf person in Tanzania and one in Brazil; the eleven "language resources" on the website (English, French, Portuguese, Spanish, Swahili, Amharic, Afan Oromo, Somali, Bahasa Indonesia, and Yoruba among them) are teaching materials that gloss the symbols in a local language, not translations of the SUN text itself.

In this respect SUN belongs with ideographic and pictographic systems such as Blissymbolics rather than with braille or Moon type, and the closest analogy for a braille reader is this: imagine a braille code in which every cell was a whole word, and in which the words were the same in every country. The reading is of a sequence of symbols left to right, with verse numbers in ordinary numerals; how names, numbers, tense, plurals, and other grammar are handled is not described anywhere public, and that is one of the first things to ask.

Physically, SUN exists in two forms. The visual form is printed on paper or shown on a screen for Deaf readers who can see. The tactile form for Deaf-Blind readers is produced as raised symbols on 3D printers; no embossing, thermoform, swell-paper, or laser-cut production has been described. A braille teacher will immediately see the practical questions: how large a raised symbol must be to be distinguished by touch, how many fit on a page, how a reader keeps the line, and how fast a fluent reader can go. None of that is published.

## How it is taught

Learners study symbols paired with pictures, taught by local facilitators whom Wycliffe Associates trains in workshops, alongside the national sign language where one exists. Lessons begin in the Gospel of Mark. A free Android app, SUN Lessons, offers fourteen lessons over about a hundred basic symbols with flashcards, tests, and sentence building, in English. A 26-page English training manual exists and is being translated into other languages by volunteers. No public dictionary, primer, or symbol list was found online; a third-party site says reader and translator dictionaries exist on the organization's website, but the page it points to shows none.

## Results, as the organization reports them

Deaf learners are said to learn the core hundred symbols in about four days; Deaf-Blind learners in about sixteen (the source does not say sixteen of what). Eight Deaf adults in India learned the core and read John 3:16 in four days. A nineteen-year-old Deaf-Blind man in West Africa learned twenty symbols at a workshop and five days later read two sentences of the SUN New Testament. By January 2024 the organization counted thirty countries, nineteen of them in Africa; its project list today names thirty countries and partners in all but two, almost all of them Deaf churches and Deaf associations. Uganda's Ministry of Education adopted SUN for a three-year pilot with Deaf-Blind learners in 2023; Tanzania uses it in churches and public schools; Burundi reports 123 new Deaf students and fifteen facilitators. The program's stated goal is evangelistic as well as literary, and conversions are reported alongside literacy counts.

What is missing is any independent assessment: no literacy study, no evaluation by linguists or by educators of the Deaf or Deaf-Blind, no published critique from Deaf-Blind advocates, and no retention or reading-speed data. That is not evidence against SUN; it is the state of the evidence. A braille teacher would want to know whether SUN readers progress to braille or sign language, or stay within SUN, and nothing public says.

## Nigeria

SUN's project list includes Nigeria with the partner Voice for the Deaf Foundation and "a sustainable development plan across seven locations," Deaf churches and classrooms, with a local leader quoted on members' enthusiasm. Yoruba is one of the eleven language resources. Nothing found concerns blind Nigerians, Deaf-Blind Nigerians, or Oyo State.

## Why this still matters to Brian

Three reasons, in order of weight. First, the technology: the SUN New Testament is a 475-page PDF whose symbols are glyphs of an embedded custom font, which means a machine-readable SUN exists even though no one has published it as such; the companion report on access explains what follows from that. Second, the Deaf-Blind: these are people for whom neither audio nor ordinary braille works, and a tactile SUN that could be verified, taught, and improved by people who understand touch reading is a place where braille expertise is wanted and apparently scarce. Third, the overlap of maps: Wycliffe Associates has Deaf partners in Nigeria and a Yoruba resource; Brian has a blind network in Ibadan and Lagos taking shape. Where those meet, if they meet, is a question for the first conversation, not an assumption.

## Questions to ask about SUN

What is the full symbol inventory, and does a dictionary with glosses exist for readers and translators. How are proper names, numbers, tense, and plurals handled. What is the current state of the tactile version, what size and material are the raised symbols, and has anyone with braille-teaching experience evaluated it. Does the SUN font exist as a file, and can it be shared. Has any Deaf-Blind reader progressed to braille, and does that matter to the program. What does the Nigerian program do, where, and could a blind-services network in Oyo State be useful to it.
