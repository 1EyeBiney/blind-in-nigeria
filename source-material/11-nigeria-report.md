# Serving the Blind in Nigeria: What Is There, Where the Need Is, and Where a Small Church Fits

Prepared by Brian for Brian's church. Research completed September 15, 2026. Oyo State first, Nigeria as context.

## Why this report

Brian's church has a family connection to Oyo State through a friend from Ibadan whose family still lives in and around the city, and an instructor who teaches screen readers remotely for a living. Before the church puts money or effort into helping blind people there, this report asks the wider question: what does it actually take to serve blind people well in that part of Nigeria, what is already there, and where is the need greatest? The answer turns out to be broader than teaching technology, and it changes what the first steps should be. Seven research passes were run, each with sources checked in September 2026; the full reports and source lists are in the research files Brian keeps. Where something could not be confirmed, this report says so rather than guessing.

## The short version

There are two different worlds inside the phrase "the blind in Nigeria," and they call for different help.

The first world is people who are blind but need not be. In southwest Nigeria, cataract causes nearly half of all blindness, and the local surveys say that more than 90 percent of blindness there is avoidable or treatable. Oyo State has real capacity to fix this: a large private not-for-profit eye network in Ibadan performing about 3,300 cataract surgeries a year, a teaching hospital with an active eye department, a second teaching hospital in Ogbomoso, and a state government program with money attached. What keeps people blind is access, money, and distance, especially in rural areas. A single surgery using the low-cost technique these hospitals favor is priced around $100 by international charities, and outreach programs elsewhere in Nigeria (one teaching hospital in Benin City in 2022) have charged patients as little as $6 or $7, with sponsors covering the gap. Nobody in Oyo State publishes a sponsorship price, so a direct inquiry is needed, but this is the place where a few thousand dollars a year does the most measurable good.

The second world is people whose blindness will not be reversed: glaucoma, which about one adult in twenty has nationally, which older regional studies suggest is particularly aggressive in Yoruba populations, and which only about one in twenty sufferers knows they have; congenital and traumatic blindness in children; and everyone for whom surgery came too late. For these people Oyo State has surprisingly little that could be confirmed. There are at least four schools for blind children in the state, but no rehabilitation or vocational centre for blind adults, no technology training program, no orientation and mobility program, and no faith-based blind ministry could be found inside the state. The nearest ones are in Lagos. Blind adults without family support commonly survive by begging; a registered blind persons' association near Ibadan with over a hundred members reports exactly that. This is the world the church's people are built for: a congregation that knows what losing sight is like, an instructor who can teach a blind person to use a free screen reader from four thousand miles away, and a family on the ground.

The recommendation that follows from this is to do a little of both, in a deliberate order: learn first through friends in Ibadan, give one small verifiable gift to an established eye hospital to start the relationship and restore someone's sight, and build the slower, more personal work of equipping and teaching one blind adult in Ibadan who can become the local hand for others.

## The people and the causes

Nigeria's only national eye survey, run in 2005 to 2007, found that about 4.2 percent of adults over forty were blind by the World Health Organization's definition, roughly ten to fifteen times the rate the CDC reports for American adults of the same ages, and estimated 1.13 million blind adults over forty in the country at that time. Cataract caused about 43 percent of that blindness nationally, glaucoma about 17 percent, corneal scarring about 8 percent, and trachoma about 4 percent, and 84 percent of it was avoidable. The southwest, including Oyo State, had the lowest blindness rate of any region, about 2.8 percent of adults over forty, with cataract responsible for 47 to 52 percent of it depending on how the zone is drawn.

The newest ground-level data for Oyo State comes from Saki East, a rural local government area about a hundred kilometers from Ibadan. A 2022 survey of adults over fifty there found 1.7 percent blind, with cataract again the leading cause and 94.6 percent of blindness avoidable. A 2024 survey of 3,671 schoolchildren in the same area found 1.4 percent visually impaired or blind, but with a different pattern: half of the impairment was uncorrected refractive error, which glasses fix, and the leading cause of actual blindness in children was cataract following an eye injury. A child with a history of eye injury was about six and a half times more likely to be blind. That is a locally specific finding worth remembering: in rural Oyo, child eye injury and missing eyeglasses matter more than the national numbers would suggest.

Glaucoma deserves its own paragraph. A national sub-study found about 5 percent of adults over forty have it, that blindness from it rises steeply with age, and that only 5.6 percent of people who have it know they do. Glaucoma is irreversible; the only defense is finding it early. This is the largest gap that needs no surgeon to begin addressing, only awareness and a referral path.

On the dust: a friend's account that many people are blind from the dust in the air reflects something real. The harmattan wind from December to February is well documented to cause dry eye, irritation, and conjunctivitis across West Africa, and dust complicates healing after injury or surgery. The medical literature does not list it as a leading cause of blindness itself; the blinding diseases are cataract, glaucoma, corneal scarring, and in rural areas river blindness. Both things can be true: the dust hurts people's eyes every year, and the reason so many stay blind is that cataracts go unoperated and glaucoma goes undetected.

How many people this means: Oyo State has roughly seven to eight million people and Ibadan about two million in the city proper and four million in the metropolitan area. Applying the survey rates to today's population, somewhere between five and twenty thousand people in Oyo State are blind from cataract, a few thousand of them in Ibadan, and at the international benchmark of about $100 a surgery it would cost on the order of a million dollars to reach them all. No urban survey of Ibadan exists, so these are ranges, not counts; the companion note "How Many People, and What Would It Cost" shows the arithmetic and every assumption.

## The needs map

For each layer of the landscape: what exists in Oyo State, what is missing, what one unit of help costs where a figure could be found, and how well it fits a small church with a few thousand dollars a year, no travel, a family on the ground, and a remote instructor.

Sight restoration and eye care. Exists: University College Hospital Ibadan (teaching hospital, active ophthalmology department); Eleta Eye Institute, a private not-for-profit network of five sites based in Ibadan that performed 16,590 cataract surgeries in five years and in 2025 opened Nigeria's first cataract surgery simulation training centre with Christian Blind Mission and HelpMeSee; LAUTECH Teaching Hospital in Ogbomoso with an ophthalmology department of unconfirmed capacity; and the state's "SightQuest" program, part of a 25 billion naira federal and state avoidable-blindness initiative, with a reported 3 billion naira allocation for Oyo. Missing: any published price for a sponsored surgery at an Oyo provider, any data on backlog or wait times, and any confirmation that these institutions accept small earmarked donations. Unit cost: market price for cataract surgery in Nigeria runs roughly 150,000 to 1,000,000 naira ($95 to $650); a subsidized outreach in Benin City charged patients 10,000 naira ($6 to $7); the international charity benchmark is $100 per surgery. Fit: high for the church's dollars, low for its unique skills. Accountability is easy because the hospital's own billing is the record. The church cannot verify who receives a surgery without a named contact and a simple reporting agreement.

Education of blind children. Exists: four schools for blind or deaf-blind children in Oyo State were identified in a peer-reviewed survey: in Ibadan (Aperin Oniyere), Oyo town (Doba), Eruwa, and Ogbomoso, the last being the largest and state-connected, with 86 students across all four at the time of the survey, mostly older teens and young adults, three-quarters male. The Federal College of Education (Special) in Oyo town trains special-education teachers and describes itself as having the largest concentration of disabled students and specialized facilities in Nigeria. Missing: current enrollment, what a year costs a family, braille availability, and any Christian mission school for the blind inside Oyo State; the clear Christian model, Pacelli School in Lagos, offers free primary education and seeks sponsors for secondary schooling. A 2021 child-safety incident at the Ogbomoso centre, with staff suspended, is on record and its resolution is unconfirmed. Why children stay out of school nationally: poverty deep enough that some families abandon a blind child, near-absence of braille materials, stigma that treats blindness as a curse, and academic struggle without support. Unit cost: not confirmed for any Oyo school; Pacelli's secondary sponsorship amount is not published. Fit: moderate. Sponsoring a student's secondary schooling is a real, documented model, but naming and choosing a child is criticized in the missions literature for creating division and dependence; funding a school's stated need (braille materials, a term's fees for whoever the school names) avoids that.

Adult rehabilitation, independent living, and livelihoods. Exists: a state disability agency (OYSAPD, created December 2022, with an office at the state secretariat in Ibadan and a public email and phone); an active state chapter of the national disability umbrella body JONAPWD; a registered blind persons' association in the Hausa community at Sabo and Moniya near Ibadan with over a hundred members; a 2018 national disability law and an Oyo State disability law. Missing: any rehabilitation or vocational training centre for blind adults inside Oyo State (the national ones, the Federal Nigeria Society for the Blind and the Resource Centre for the Blind, are in Lagos), any white-cane or orientation and mobility program in the state, any disability-inclusive microfinance. The association near Ibadan reports its members survive mainly by begging and cannot pay their children's school fees. Unit cost: not confirmed for a cane, a training slot, or a microloan in Oyo State. Fit: high for relationship-based help through an existing association, low for anything requiring a building.

Assistive technology and digital access. Exists: NVDA, free; budget Android phones with TalkBack at roughly 100,000 to 200,000 naira ($65 to $130) new; monthly data at roughly 3,000 to 7,500 naira ($2 to $5) for 7 to 20 gigabytes; the University of Ibadan's special education department has a visual impairment specialization. Training organizations exist in Lagos (the Anglo-Nigerian Welfare Association for the Blind's computer training unit, Bethesda Home and School for the Blind). Missing: any confirmed technology training for blind people inside Oyo State, and any confirmed remote training program reaching Nigeria from abroad, though Hadley in Illinois offers free remote courses worldwide. JAWS is reported at around 900,000 naira in Nigeria, out of reach for nearly everyone. Electricity works most of the time for about 13 percent of Nigerians (Afrobarometer), so lessons must be phone-first, short, and downloadable. Fit: the highest in this report for the church's unique skills. One phone, one year of data, one solar bank, and Brian's teaching can take a motivated blind adult in Ibadan from owning a phone to using it independently, and then to teaching others.

Faith-based work. Exists: Yoruba audio Scripture, free, from the Bible Society of Nigeria and Biblica through the Bible.com and Bible.is apps; Pacelli School in Lagos (Catholic); Bethesda Church in Lagos, an independent congregation led and staffed almost entirely by blind people, whose school and vocational training are funded partly by craft sales, which is a striking parallel to the church's own plan; Christian Blind Mission, active in Nigeria and a partner in the Ibadan training centre, though its Nigeria office location is unconfirmed; the Nigerian Baptist Convention's historic institutions in Ogbomoso. Missing: any blind ministry inside Oyo State, any Yoruba braille Scripture in confirmed circulation, and any Nigeria presence for Torch Trust or Christian Record Services (their African work is elsewhere). Unit cost: audio Scripture is free to anyone with a phone. Fit: high. Putting the Yoruba audio Bible on a blind person's phone in Ibadan is the cheapest, most immediate, most explicitly gospel act in this whole report.

Partnering and money. What works at one to five thousand dollars a year: paying an established institution for a defined unit of service, where its own receipt is the record; supporting one already-trusted local worker with a fixed stipend and simple check-ins; church-to-church relationships sustained by direct contact; and diaspora-led giving through a family member on the ground, which the literature treats as one of the more reliable channels because the person moving the money has their own reputation at stake. What fails: responding to urgency and crisis appeals (the classic advance-fee pattern, now written by AI), funding individuals with no reporting trail, and the foreign side deciding alone who gets helped. Legal side: the church needs nothing new; a Nigerian organization that will receive sustained foreign funds needs Corporate Affairs Commission registration as an incorporated trustee (roughly 115,000 to 205,000 naira with legal help, about $75 to $135, or less without) and free registration with the Special Control Unit Against Money Laundering before banks will comfortably move donated money. An individual helper paid a stipend, or an existing hospital or school paid for a service, needs neither. Bank-to-bank transfers cost about 2.7 percent on average (World Bank remittance price data). A part-time helper in Ibadan can be supported for a modest monthly amount; community-reported cost-of-living data (Numbeo) puts the average net salary in Ibadan at roughly $50 to $55.

## Ways the church could help, ranked

These are ranked by a combination of how much good a dollar does, how well the work fits the church's actual people, and how safely it can be done from Indianapolis. They are not exclusive; the sequence at the end puts them in order.

First, learn before spending. Send friends in Ibadan the interview guide at the end of this report and ask them to spend a few weeks talking to blind people, the association at Sabo and Moniya, the state disability agency, and one of the eye hospitals. Almost every "could not confirm" in this report is a question a person in Ibadan can answer in an afternoon. Cost: a few hundred naira in transport and airtime. This is the step that turns a plan into a relationship.

Second, restore someone's sight as the first gift. After a direct inquiry to Eleta Eye Institute (or UCH's eye department) about whether they accept a small donation earmarked for surgeries for patients who cannot pay, and at what price, fund a defined number: five surgeries at the $100 benchmark is $500. Ask for a count and a photo or note of the outreach, not a general thank-you. This is the highest measurable good per dollar in the report, the easiest to account for, and the kind of concrete outcome a congregation can understand: "we restored sight for five people this year." It does not use the church's unique skills, and it is a transaction rather than a relationship, which is why it is the first gift and not the whole plan.

Third, equip and teach one person, then let them teach. Through friends in Ibadan, find one blind adult in Ibadan who wants to learn and one trusted sighted adult nearby. Fund a budget Android phone, a year of data, and a solar power bank, roughly $150 to $250 once. Brian teaches phone accessibility and NVDA remotely by short recorded lessons and WhatsApp voice notes, with the sighted helper keeping the device charged and reporting back. Put the Yoruba audio Bible on the phone in the first week. When the learner is competent, the goal shifts: they become the local trainer, with two or three learners of their own, and a modest stipend for that work becomes the church's recurring support. This is the piece nobody else in Oyo State is doing, it fills a confirmed gap, and it is exactly what the church has that other churches do not. It is slow, it depends on power and connectivity, and it depends on friends in Ibadan; those are its honest limits.

Fourth, put Yoruba audio Scripture in blind hands. This costs nothing and can start the day someone in Ibadan has a phone. It is also the most direct gospel act available and needs no partner, no registration, and no money.

Fifth, support an existing association's named need. The blind persons' association at Sabo and Moniya, or whoever JONAPWD's Oyo chapter points to, named their own unmet need: their children's school fees. A small, recurring gift for a need the members themselves named, given through friends in Ibadan with receipts, is relationship-based help that respects the people receiving it. Before any sustained giving to an organization, check that it holds CAC and SCUML registration; for a one-time gift for a specific purpose, a receipt and a photo suffice.

Sixth, fund a school's need rather than a child. Once friends in Ibadan have visited the Ibadan school at Aperin Oniyere or the centre in Oyo town and learned what a term costs and what the school lacks, fund that: braille materials, or a term's fees for whichever student the school names. Ask about child safety directly, given the 2021 incident at the Ogbomoso centre. Avoid running a named-child sponsorship program.

Seventh, glaucoma awareness through the local helper. Only one in twenty Nigerians with glaucoma knows it. A helper who can tell people over forty with a family history to get their eye pressure checked at UCH or Eleta, and who knows what the outreach days are, is doing real prevention at no cost. This needs a clinical partner for screening; the church's part is the telling.

What the church should not do: build or run a school, a clinic, or a training centre; buy JAWS licenses at Nigerian prices when NVDA is free; register its own Nigerian nonprofit for a program this size; run a named-child sponsorship program; or send money in response to a crisis story, however sincere it sounds. All of these either duplicate what larger organizations already do at scale, or match the documented ways small foreign efforts fail.

## A suggested sequence

Months one to three: the interview guide goes to friends in Ibadan; they report back; the church writes to Eleta Eye Institute and UCH with two questions (do you accept small earmarked donations for indigent surgeries, and at what price per surgery). The church's treasurer or CPA reviews a one-page overseas support agreement.

Months three to six: first gift, five surgeries or whatever the inquiry supports; first learner and helper identified; phone, data, and solar bank funded; lessons begin; Yoruba audio Bible installed. A decision point: is the learner progressing, and is the helper reporting?

Months six to twelve: the learner begins teaching one or two others; a stipend begins; a first small gift to the association's named need, or to a school's named need. A second decision point: has a relationship formed that would survive those friends stepping back?

Year two: if the answer is yes, consider whether the local group needs to become an organization (CAC and SCUML registration, six to eight weeks, under $150), and whether proceeds from the church's own ministry can support a second learner or a second helper. If the answer is no, the church has still restored sight for a handful of people and taught one person to use a phone, and it has learned what it needs to know.

Public email info@oysapd.ng, phone +234 913 500 2022, site oysapd.ng. Schools for the blind in Oyo State: Aperin Oniyere (Ibadan), Doba (Oyo town), the Ogbomoso training centre, and the school for the deaf and blind at Eruwa. University of Ibadan, Department of Special Education, visual impairment unit. Site educ.ui.edu.ng. Ask: is there assistive technology support for blind students, and who in Ibadan trains blind adults in technology, if anyone?

Nigeria Association of the Blind. National self-advocacy body; Lagos and Abuja chapters confirmed, Oyo chapter unconfirmed. Site nigeriaassociationoftheblind.org. Ask whether an Oyo chapter exists.

Resource Centre for the Blind, Yaba, Lagos. Rehabilitation, vocational training, and assistive technology training. Site resourcecenterfortheblind.org. Ask: cost and admission for a training slot for an adult from Oyo State.

Anglo-Nigerian Welfare Association for the Blind (ANWAB). Computer training unit teaching screen readers; location of the unit unconfirmed. Site anwabnigeria.org.

Pacelli School for the Blind and Partially Sighted Children, Surulere, Lagos. Catholic residential school, free primary education, seeks sponsors for secondary. Site pacellischoolfortheblind.org. A model to study and a possible sponsorship channel if nothing in Oyo State fits.

Bethesda Church and Bethesda Home for the Blind, Idi Oro, Lagos. Blind-led congregation and school funded partly by craft sales. No public contact page found. A parallel to the church's own model, worth a visit by anyone in Lagos.

Christian Blind Mission (CBM). International Christian-heritage disability and eye-health organization active in Nigeria and a partner at the Ibadan training centre; Nigeria office location unconfirmed. Site cbm.org.

Bible Society of Nigeria. Publisher of the Yoruba "Bibeli Mimọ," available in audio through Bible.com and Bible.is.

Nigerian Baptist Theological Seminary, Ogbomoso. Historic Baptist institution in Oyo State; no blind ministry confirmed, but a natural place to ask whether any congregation nearby has one.

Hadley, Winnetka, Illinois. Free remote courses for blind adults worldwide, including technology; a resource to point a Nigerian learner toward, or a model.

In Indianapolis: Flair of Hope Church, an Assemblies of God church plant for Africans in Indianapolis, and possibly Ebenezer Indy (details unconfirmed). Either could be a source of Yoruba-speaking friends and advice.

## Interview guide for friends in Ibadan

These are questions for conversations in Ibadan, in whatever language is natural. They are meant for talking to blind people, their families, the association at Sabo and Moniya, the disability agency, a school, and an eye hospital. Not every question is for every person. Voice notes back over WhatsApp are fine; nothing needs to be written down formally. The goal is to understand, not to promise anything.

About blind people you know. Who do you know who is blind or losing their sight? How old are they? When did it start, and what do they say caused it? Did they ever see a doctor about it, and what happened? Do they know whether it could be fixed with surgery?

About eye care. Where do people here go when their eyes fail? Has anyone you know had cataract surgery, and where, and what did it cost them? Have you heard of free or cheap eye surgery days at UCH, Eleta, or anywhere else? Do people know about glaucoma, and does anyone get their eye pressure checked?

About children. Do you know blind children who are not in school? Why are they not in school? What would it cost for a child to attend the school for the blind in Ibadan, and does the school have braille books? How do families feel about a blind child?

About adults. How do blind adults here earn money or eat? Do any beg? Do any work? Has anyone ever taught a blind adult to walk with a cane or to do things for themselves? Is there any place a blind adult can go for training?

About phones and power. Does the blind person you know have a phone, and can they use it? Do they know a phone can talk? How often is there electricity in their home, and how do they charge things? What does data cost them a month?

About community and faith. Is there a group or association of blind people here? Who leads it? What do they say they need most? Does any church here have blind members or do anything for them? Would a blind person want to hear the Bible in Yoruba on their phone?

About helping. If a church in America wanted to help blind people here with a small amount of money, what would help most? What would be a waste? Is there someone here, honest and patient, who would want to learn to use a phone without sight and then teach others? Is there someone who could sit with a blind learner and keep a phone charged?

About you. Which of these things would you be willing to do, and what would make it hard?

## What is still unconfirmed

The per-surgery sponsorship price at any Oyo State provider and whether they accept small earmarked gifts. Current enrollment and cost at the four Oyo State schools, and the resolution of the 2021 incident at the Ogbomoso centre. Whether any technology training, rehabilitation centre, or blind ministry exists inside Oyo State without a web presence. Whether the Nigeria Association of the Blind has an Oyo chapter. CBM's Nigeria office location. Current official fee schedules for CAC and SCUML registration. The realistic stipend for a helper in Ibadan, beyond the cost-of-living data. Nearly all of these are answerable by a person in Ibadan, which is why the first step is the interview guide.
