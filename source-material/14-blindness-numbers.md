# How Many People, and What Would It Cost: Blindness and Cataract in Nigeria, Oyo State, and Ibadan

Prepared by Brian for Brian's church, September 15, 2026. A numbers note to accompany "Serving the Blind in Nigeria." Every input is named so anyone can check or change it.

## What was corrected first

Three things needed fixing before any arithmetic.

The comparison with the United States. The draft paragraph said the CDC estimates 0.8 percent of Americans in the same age range are blind or visually impaired. That figure matches neither CDC number. The CDC's Vision and Eye Health Surveillance System puts blindness among Americans aged 40 to 64 at 0.21 percent and among those 65 to 84 at 0.43 percent, and puts any vision loss among those 40 to 64 at 9.84 percent. The definitions also differ: the CDC counts blindness as best-corrected acuity of 20/200 or worse in the better eye, while the Nigerian survey counts presenting acuity worse than 3/60 (roughly 20/400) in the better eye, without glasses the person does not own. The Nigerian threshold is stricter, so the true gap is even larger than the raw numbers suggest. The fair sentence is: about 4.2 percent of Nigerian adults over forty were blind by the survey's definition, against roughly a quarter to half a percent of American adults in the same ages by the CDC's; put another way, ten to fifteen times the rate, for a condition mostly caused by something a short surgery fixes.

The cataract share in the southwest. The draft said about 47 percent. The survey reports two geographies: 46.6 percent for the rainforest ecological zone and 52.3 percent for the South West geopolitical zone, which is the one Oyo State belongs to. Both are correct; the note below uses the range 47 to 52 percent.

The survey's own count. The 2005 to 2007 survey estimated 1.13 million blind adults aged forty and over in Nigeria at that time, with a confidence range of 1.03 to 1.25 million, and found cataract blindness in 1.8 percent of adults over forty. Those two figures, not just the percentages, anchor the national estimate.

## Nigeria

Adults aged 40 and over: 38 to 48 million, central 43 million. Blind by the survey definition: 1.6 to 2.0 million, central 1.8 million. That is higher than the survey's 1.13 million in 2007 only because the population has grown; whether the rate itself has fallen since then is unknown, since no newer national survey exists. Blind from cataract: 680,000 to 870,000, central about 780,000. At $100 a surgery, restoring sight to everyone in that group would cost roughly $68 to $87 million, central $78 million; at $150, about $100 to $130 million. That is the size of the national problem, and it belongs to governments and the large organizations, not to a small church; it is included so the local numbers have a frame.

## Oyo State

Two methods, because they bracket the answer.

Using the national survey's southwest figures: adults 40 and over in Oyo State, 1.1 to 1.6 million, central 1.35 million. Blind: 31,000 to 45,000, central about 38,000. Blind from cataract: 15,000 to 23,000, central about 19,000. Cost to clear that backlog at $100 a surgery: $1.5 to $2.3 million, central $1.9 million.

Using the 2022 Saki East survey, which is newer and local but rural and covers only people over fifty: adults 50 and over in Oyo State, 700,000 to 960,000. Blind: 12,000 to 16,000. Blind from cataract: 4,500 to 6,200. Cost at $100: about $450,000 to $620,000.

So the honest answer for Oyo State is somewhere between about 5,000 and 20,000 people blind from cataract, with a cost to restore all of their sight between roughly half a million and two million dollars at the benchmark price. The lower figure reflects newer data and probably better surgical access than in 2007; the higher figure includes people in their forties whom the Saki survey did not count. For planning, "about ten thousand people in Oyo State are blind from cataract, and it would take on the order of a million dollars to reach them all" is defensible and should be stated as a range, not a count.

A useful comparison: Eleta Eye Institute alone performs about 3,300 cataract surgeries a year in Oyo State. Against a backlog of 5,000 to 20,000, that is between one and a half and six years of one provider's capacity, before counting new cases. The bottleneck is not surgeons; it is finding the people, getting them to the hospital, and paying for those who cannot pay, which is exactly the gap a sponsor and a local helper fill.

## Ibadan

Using the survey method: adults 40 and over, 340,000 for the city proper up to 820,000 for the metropolitan area; blind, 9,000 to 23,000; blind from cataract, 4,400 to 12,000; cost at $100, $440,000 to $1.2 million. Using the Saki East method: blind from cataract, 1,300 to 3,200; cost, $135,000 to $320,000. Urban Ibadan probably has better access to surgery than rural Saki East and a different age mix, so the truth for the city is likely toward the lower end of the survey method, on the order of a few thousand people blind from cataract, and a few hundred thousand dollars to reach them all.

## What the numbers do not say

They count people blind in both eyes. Far more people are blind in one eye from cataract, and many more have visual impairment short of blindness; the survey's 84 percent "avoidable" figure includes those and includes refractive error and trachoma, which $100 surgeries do not address. Not everyone counted will accept surgery or be fit for it; uptake studies across Nigeria find fear, cost, distance, and the belief that nothing can be done keep many from operating rooms even when surgery is free. The numbers are a backlog, a stock of people blind today, and each year adds new cases; the survey did not estimate the annual rate, and Nigeria's cataract surgical rate could not be confirmed this pass. The prevalence rates are from 2007 and may have improved; the population figures are estimates; the 40-and-over share is an assumption. The $100 is a benchmark, not a quote from any Oyo hospital.

## What a small church's money means against these numbers

Five surgeries a year, at the benchmark $100, is $500 and is a rounding error against a backlog of ten thousand. It is also five people who see again, in a place where the constraint is money and reach rather than surgeons. Fifty surgeries a year, $5,000, would still be half a percent of the Oyo backlog. The right claim for the congregation is not that the church will solve cataract blindness in Oyo State but that each hundred dollars, given through a real provider with a real receipt, is one person's sight.

## What would tighten these numbers

A direct figure for the share of Nigerians and of Oyo State residents aged 40 and over, from the National Population Commission or the UN population database. Oyo State's latest official population projection. A price per sponsored surgery from Eleta Eye Institute or UCH Ibadan, which replaces the benchmark. Nigeria's current cataract surgical rate, from the IAPB Vision Atlas or a recent paper, to turn the backlog into a yearly picture. Whether the Saki East survey used the same blindness definition as the national survey. Each of these is a single lookup or a single question to a hospital.

## Corrected paragraph for the report

Nigeria's only national eye survey, run in 2005 to 2007, found that about 4.2 percent of adults over forty were blind by the World Health Organization's definition, roughly ten to fifteen times the rate the CDC reports for American adults of the same ages, and estimated 1.13 million blind adults over forty in the country at that time. Cataract caused about 43 percent of that blindness nationally, glaucoma about 17 percent, corneal scarring about 8 percent, and trachoma about 4 percent, and 84 percent of it was avoidable. The southwest, including Oyo State, had the lowest blindness rate of any region, about 2.8 percent of adults over forty, with cataract responsible for 47 to 52 percent of it depending on how the zone is drawn. Applying those rates to today's population, somewhere between five and twenty thousand people in Oyo State are blind from cataract, a few thousand of them in Ibadan, and at the international benchmark of about $100 a surgery it would cost on the order of a million dollars to reach them all; a separate note shows the arithmetic and its assumptions.
