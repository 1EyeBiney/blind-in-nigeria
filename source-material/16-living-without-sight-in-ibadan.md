# Living Without Sight in Ibadan

What life is like for blind people in the part of Nigeria a friend of Brian's comes from, told from documented accounts. Prepared by Brian for Brian's church, September 18, 2026.

## Before you read

Everything in this piece comes from a published interview, a news report, a peer-reviewed study, or an organization's own account, and every person named here was named in public by the source. Nothing is composite and nothing is invented. Where the account is from Lagos rather than Ibadan, it says so. Where I could not confirm something, I say that too. I have written it this way because you will repeat it to people who trust you, and because the people in it deserve to be described as they are: not as objects of pity, but as neighbors living with dignity inside conditions that a hundred dollars and a phone can change.

## A city of two million

Ibadan is the capital of Oyo State, an hour or two by road from Lagos, and the largest city in the region that friend comes from. About two million people live in the city itself and four million in the wider area. Applying Nigeria's national eye survey to those numbers, somewhere between four and twelve thousand adults in the Ibadan area are blind from cataract alone, and thousands more from glaucoma and other causes. The survey found that about 4.2 percent of Nigerian adults over forty were blind, ten to twenty times the rate for Americans of the same ages, and that about half of that blindness in the southwest comes from cataract, which a short surgery can reverse. Those are the numbers. What follows is what the numbers look like from the roadside.

## Rahinatu, at the roadside in Oke Bola

Rahinatu Ibrahim, called Ganga, is visually impaired and has begged in the Oke Bola and Oke Ado areas of Ibadan for thirty years. She came from Kano State in the north after both of her husbands, who were also blind, died and left her with seven children. "I came here to look for food to feed my children," she told the Nigerian Tribune. "I was left with the children, after my two husbands died." For years she and her children begged together across the city. Leg pain eventually made her stationary: "I just come to the roadside and sit to beg." She sent her daughters back north to be married rather than let them marry in Ibadan. Of the city she says: "Since I came here, no one has ever harassed or taken advantage of me. The people here have been good to me."

That is the shape of it. A blind woman, widowed twice, raises seven children on what people drop in her hand at the roadside, for three decades, and speaks well of the city that let her.

## The association at Sabo, and what they do at night

Ibadan's blind Hausa community organized itself. Mohammed Ibrahim chairs the Blind Hausa People's Association of Oyo State, based in the Sabo neighborhood, registered with the government, with more members at Moniya and elsewhere in the state. "We have over 100 members here in Sabo and all of them have an identity card issued by the association," he told the Tribune. The association is run entirely by blind people, with one sighted secretary kept on "because he will be writing for us."

When the state moved members out of Sabo, he said, "Since we were moved, our situation really changed. We can no longer afford our children's school fees." Asked what the association contributes to the city, he did not talk about begging. "Our greatest contributions are our prayers. If you come here in the night you would meet over 100 of us praying."

A hundred blind people, praying together at night, in a neighborhood in Ibadan. Whatever else is true about their lives, that is also true.

## The king of the beggars, and the state's answer

In June 2021, the governor of Oyo State moved more than three thousand beggars, many of them blind, from Sabo to a new resettlement centre at Akinyele with boreholes, toilets, solar power, and state-provided meals. Among them was Alhaji Ibrahim Mohammed, whom Legit.ng called the king of the blind beggars of Oyo State, a man with three wives and many children. "This place is good and better, compared to where we were staying before," he said. "It has facilities which have made life easier for us." Then he asked the governor for three things: separate rooms for married couples, backup electricity when the public supply fails, and a school for the children living there.

Notice what he asked for. Privacy for married people, reliable power, and school for the children. Those are the requests of a father and a husband, not of a beggar.

## The preacher who memorized the Bible

Isaac Olayinka is a blind clergyman at Christ Apostolic Church in Ibadan. He preaches regularly, and he needs someone to guide him through the crowded roads to get to each engagement. He has built his ministry on memory. "For a long time, I have mastered how to cram the bible on a daily basis, after people must have read any particular portion to me," he told Sahara Reporters in 2019.

The reporter told him that phones and computers can read Scripture aloud to a blind person. He had never heard of it. "I have never heard that computer and phone can help one read the bible or other Christian books on one's own as a blind," he said. "You are the first to tell me so."

A working preacher in a city of two million had never been told that his phone could read him the Bible. The Yoruba audio Bible is free, on any phone, today. That gap, between what exists and what people know exists, is the gap the church is best placed to close.

## A family in Lagos that refused to beg

Not every blind adult begs, and the ones who do not are worth knowing. In Meiran, on the edge of Lagos, the Opebiyis are a blind couple with two sighted children, Aanuoluwapo and Oluwasemilogo, who were seven and five when Vanguard visited in 2017. The headline was the family's own rule: begging for alms is a taboo. Their children are not used as guides. "I recognize them by voice and feeling," the mother said. They chose a nearby private school over the free public one because "the nearest public school to us is on the other side of the express-way, so, for safety reason, we decided to enroll them in a nearby private school." A neighbor said of the parents: "They wake up before everybody to attend to their children."

## Crossing the street

John Adoyi is blind, a journalist, and a graduate of Pacelli School in Lagos. In January 2026 he wrote for TechCabal about a day he forgot his white cane and tried to cross Lagos with a navigation app instead. At a busy expressway transit point he waited thirteen minutes for a stranger to help him cross, because "the app could not tell me when it was safe to cross." Drivers, he wrote, rarely recognize a white cane as a signal that its holder means to cross, and the audible pedestrian signals common elsewhere are nearly nonexistent in Nigeria. Of the cane itself: "To a blind person, it is sight. It tells me when the ground slopes, where a gutter is, how close a body is, and whether it is safe to keep moving." Of an open roadside drain: "I wished the app could tell me how deep the drain was."

And of why he goes out at all: "independence is not optional for us. If we waited for perfect conditions, we would never leave our homes."

That account is from Lagos, not Ibadan, and I could not find its equivalent written from Ibadan. But the open drains, the missing sidewalks, and the drivers are the same across Nigerian cities, and no white-cane or mobility training program could be found anywhere in Oyo State.

## What the research says about all of this

A 2010 study of sixty-one totally blind adults in Ilorin, a Nigerian city north of Ibadan, put numbers on the pattern. Seventy-seven percent supported themselves by street begging, yet eighty-nine percent wanted a different occupation. About eighty percent were married, usually to another blind person, and two thirds had five or more children; the authors wrote that blind adults "enjoyed a reasonable degree of family life and relationships." Eighty-one percent needed help to move around, most often from a spouse. Blindness, the study found, "still evokes negative attitudes and some degree of stigma in society," and nearly half of the people interviewed feared their own children would go blind. Half showed signs of psychological distress. Twenty-eight percent said they were happy, which the authors credited in part to faith.

Begging in Nigeria is not a personal failing. It is what happens when a country has no rehabilitation centres for blind adults in a state of seven million people, no mobility training, no technology training, and electricity that works most of the time for one person in eight. It is the rational thing to do when nobody has shown you anything else.

## The children

Some blind children in Nigeria are abandoned. That is not an inference; it is what the principal of the best-known school for blind children in the country says out loud. Sister Agnes Onwudiwe of Pacelli School for the Blind in Lagos told America Magazine in November 2025: "Some of them were abandoned and people brought them to Pacelli. Many of the students say Pacelli is their home because they don't have a father and mother."

Gbolahun Azeez lost his sight suddenly as a boy. "They could not detect anything. It was so devastating [because] there was nothing wrong with me the night before," he said. "After becoming blind, I could no longer cope. I just thought all hope was lost." He is at university now. "But Pacelli changed that mentality. They renewed my hope."

Chinedu Nkwocha arrived at Pacelli at fourteen unable to speak English. "Now I can express myself [in English]," he said. "Pacelli brought out the gift and talent in me."

Cobhams Asuquo, the musician and producer who is probably Nigeria's most famous blind person, was born blind into a family of six children, none of the others blind. "Since I've never experienced sight, I wasn't aware that I was without sight," he has said. "I ran downstairs, jumped over gutters, played hard. Fought even harder." He was warned on leaving school "that we would come across people who be genuinely mean to us." He tells his own story in an eighteen-minute talk called "The Gift of Blindness," which a congregation can watch.

Oyo State has four schools for blind children, in Ibadan, Oyo town, Eruwa, and Ogbomoso, with eighty-six students among them when a study in the Nigerian Journal of Ophthalmology last counted (the date of that count is not stated). I could not find a single published first-person account from any of them. The children of Oyo State who are blind have, as far as the public record goes, never been asked.

## Two children who got their sight back

Both of these stories are from northern Nigeria, far from Ibadan, and are told by Sightsavers, the charity that paid for the surgeries; there is no comparable published story from Oyo State, though Ibadan's eye hospitals do this work every week.

Khadijah, nine, in Sokoto, had a cataract in her left eye. Her mother, a teacher who had been trained to screen for eye conditions, noticed it. The surgery took about an hour. She can see, and the account says she now hopes to become a doctor.

Muhammad, in Kaduna, was born with cataracts in both eyes and diagnosed at four. "He runs into things without seeing," his father Abubakar said before the operation. "His condition has declined drastically." Two years later Muhammad said: "I feel happy at school. I love to colour, write and draw. I want to learn how to ride a bicycle."

That is what a cataract surgery is. In Nigeria, done the low-cost way that Ibadan's hospitals already use, international charities price it at about a hundred dollars.

## Why it stays this way

Everything above rests on the same few facts. In Oyo State there is real capacity to remove cataracts (one Ibadan eye network alone does about 3,300 a year), but no published way for a poor person to get a sponsored surgery, and the people who need it most are rural, elderly, and afraid. For those whose blindness cannot be reversed, there is no adult rehabilitation centre, no cane training, no technology training, and no church ministry to blind people that could be found inside the state. Electricity works most of the time for about thirteen percent of Nigerians. A free screen reader exists, and a free Yoruba audio Bible exists, and a blind preacher in Ibadan had never heard of either.

## What a hundred dollars and a phone can do

A hundred dollars, given to an Ibadan eye hospital through a proper channel, is one person's sight. A budget phone, a year of data, and a solar charger, about two hundred dollars, plus lessons from someone who teaches screen readers for a living, is one blind adult who can read, call, learn, and hear the Bible in Yoruba without anyone's help, and who can then teach the next person. Brian's friends live there. That is not a program. It is a beginning.

## The road between Ibadan and Lagos

Because the two organizations we have looked at are in Lagos, it matters whether someone from Ibadan can get there and back. They can. A modern railway runs from Moniya station in Ibadan to Lagos in about two and a half hours; an economy seat costs 3,000 to 3,600 naira, under three dollars, and a first-class seat about 9,000 naira, under seven. Trains leave Ibadan at eight in the morning and return from Lagos at four in the afternoon, which allows a working day in Lagos with about five hours on the ground. The road alternative, the Lagos–Ibadan Expressway, is cheaper by bus (3,000 to 6,000 naira) and quoted at one to two hours, but that is the free-flowing figure; congestion and roadworks commonly stretch it, and the train is the more predictable choice. Petrol in September 2026 costs 1,350 to 1,450 naira a litre, a little over a dollar, which is why a private car is not the way ordinary people make this trip. A sighted helper's same-day round trip from Ibadan to Surulere and back, by train plus local taxis, comes to roughly ten to fourteen thousand naira, seven to ten dollars, and a full day. Inside Ibadan, a taxi starts at about 1,000 naira and runs about 1,300 a mile, so a blind adult's trip to a hospital or church across town is a few dollars each way; shared minibuses and tricycle taxis cost less, though I could not confirm their fares. What a blind person faces on those streets is what John Adoyi described in Lagos: no sidewalks to speak of, open drains, and drivers who do not stop for a cane.

## A closing word

The people in this piece are not waiting to be rescued. Rahinatu raised seven children. Mohammed Ibrahim runs an association. Alhaji Ibrahim asked for a school. Isaac Olayinka memorized the Bible. The Opebiyis got up before everyone else. John Adoyi crossed the road. Gbolahun Azeez is at university. What they lack is not courage or faith. It is a hundred dollars, a phone, and someone who knows how to use it.
